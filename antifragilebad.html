Antifragile bad

October 28, 2026

"Antifragility is beyond resilience or robustness. The resilient resists shocks and stays the same; the antifragile gets better."

- Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Antifragile (2012)

This makes no sense, right? Do you get better if someone zaps you with a taser? But here's what he actually means:

  • Fragile means that adversity is harmful.
  • Robust means that adversity doesn't change things.
  • Antifragile means that adversity is beneficial within some limits.

Now we're getting somewhere.

It's easy to read this and conclude, "Antifragile is the best thing. I should seek out antifragility." Nope! Having antifragility is good. Aiming for it is bad.

Fragility and robustness

Imagine a priceless Ming vase. If you drop it, it shatters, and a pile of porcelain shards is not as good as a vase. That's fragility.

Is fragility intrinsically bad? Maybe the vase would be better if it was made of metal? Obviously not. Fragility is just a property of an object.

Compare that to something robust, like a pile of poop. If you drop the poop, it remains poop. Is robustness good or bad? Again, it's just a property of an object.

Things get more interesting when we consider the system as a whole. Let's say we have a production line that needs Ming vases and poops. We'd need to be careful about the Ming vases, but we could mostly ignore the poops, so most of our attention would be on the vases.

That leads to our first conclusion: Robust parts of a system give you more slack.

In the real world, there's a cost to robustness — often the trade-off is that robust things are more expensive or less efficient. And there's a cost to antifragility, too.

Aiming for antifragility is not worth it

When you exercise, you're stressing and damaging your muscles so that they antifragilely become stronger. Good. You lift heavy weights and your bones thicken under the load. Good. You get a vaccine and your immune system improves under the attack. Good.

These are examples of antifragility: some amount of adversity benefits them. But we must not conclude from this that more antifragility is good. In most real-world cases, the cost of additional antifragility is too high.

You're a bodybuilder. Like all bodybuilders, you care about muscles and you educate yourself about antifragility. You realize that you should make your muscles more antifragile, and you know the best way to do that: anabolic steroids.

You take the steroids, and your muscles are so antifragile now. You can do way more training and become super strong.

Let's ignore the damage to your heart and your liver and your kidneys. And it's not just your organs; everyone else now needs to damage them to remain competitive.

Congratulations on your antifragile muscles! Meanwhile, the robust bodybuilder trains naturally, doesn't get injured, and keeps going into old age.

Antifragile finance

Okay, finance! Imagine if your investments lost money, but antifragility meant that you didn't lose money. This actually exists.

One way that financial traders get antifragility is to make huge bets with their firm's money. Punt billions on natural gas. If it goes your way, you get a $100 million bonus! If it doesn't work out, oops, your fund takes a $6.6 billion loss and you need to find another job, what a disaster for you.

Taleb says this is the "Bob Rubin trade" and that it's bad: people gain antifragility at the expense of others, and this is an ethical problem. But this is just what happens when people try to benefit from adversity! You can't just handwave the cost away by saying, tut tut, this is not cricket!

What should we do with antifragility

I believe that we should not aim for antifragility. The costs of doing so are too high and are too hard to avoid. The costs associated with robustness are way lower, and it already hardens you against negative events.

Here's a haiku I wrote about antifragility and robustness:

Aiming for antifragility is usually bad,

But use the antifragility if you have it.

Otherwise, just try to be robust.
